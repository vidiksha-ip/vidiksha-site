The examination report lands and the objection reads something like this: "The mark is devoid of distinctive character and is not capable of distinguishing the goods of one person from those of others. Objection is raised under Section 9(1)(a) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999."

Most replies I see do one of two things. They paste a paragraph about how the mark is "inherently distinctive and coined", or they attach a stack of invoices with no argument on top. Neither works alone. Here is the structure that does.

## First, read which limb you are actually facing

Section 9(1) has three limbs, and they call for different replies.

- **9(1)(a)**: devoid of distinctive character. The examiner thinks the mark cannot function as a badge of origin at all. Common for single letters, numerals, surnames and laudatory words.
- **9(1)(b)**: descriptive. The mark describes kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin or time of production. FRESH BAKE for bread. MUMBAI CABS for taxi services.
- **9(1)(c)**: customary in the current language or the established practices of the trade. The word has become the generic name for the thing.

Examiners frequently cite 9(1)(a) and 9(1)(b) together. Address them separately anyway. A reply that argues only "the mark is not descriptive" has left the 9(1)(a) objection unanswered, and that is what the hearing officer will pick on.

## The two routes, and when to take each

Every Section 9 reply travels one of two routes.

**Route one: inherent distinctiveness.** You argue the mark was never descriptive or non-distinctive to begin with. This is the right route when the mark is coined, arbitrary (an ordinary word applied to unrelated goods, like APPLE for computers), or suggestive in a way that needs a mental leap. Your job is to show the leap.

**Route two: acquired distinctiveness.** The proviso to Section 9(1) saves a mark that, before the date of application, has acquired a distinctive character through use. This is the route for descriptive marks with a real trading history. You are not arguing the examiner is wrong about the word. You are proving the public now reads it as your mark.

Pick one as your lead argument and make the other an alternative, clearly labelled as such. Do not blend them into a single paragraph that claims the mark is both "coined" and "used extensively since 2011". It reads as hedging, because it is.

## What the inherent-distinctiveness argument needs

1. Break the mark into its parts and explain what each part does. If it is a composite with a device, say what the device contributes.
2. State the goods or services precisely, then show the mark does not describe them directly. The test is whether a consumer would immediately, without thought, understand the word as describing a characteristic of the goods.
3. Cite comparable marks on the Register in the same class. Pull them from the IP India public search, give application numbers, attach the extracts. Registry practice is not binding, but it is persuasive, and examiners respond to it.
4. If the word has a dictionary meaning, deal with it. Pretending it does not exist is the fastest way to lose credibility.

## What the acquired-distinctiveness argument needs

This route lives or dies on evidence. The argument itself is two paragraphs. The annexures are the reply.

| Evidence | What it proves | The usual mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Affidavit of use | Date of first use, continuity, territory | Unsigned, undated, or sworn by someone with no personal knowledge |
| Year-wise sales figures | Scale of use under the mark | Giving company turnover rather than turnover under this mark |
| Advertising spend with samples | Public exposure to the mark as a mark | Samples that show the mark buried in descriptive running text |
| Invoices across years and cities | Actual trade, not intention | Ten invoices from one month |
| Third-party recognition | Press, awards, trade directories | Self-published material presented as independent |

Make the affidavit the spine. Every other annexure should be referred to by paragraph number in the affidavit. An examiner who can trace invoice bundle C to paragraph 7 will believe you. One who has to construct the story themselves will not.

## Structure of the reply itself

Keep it to four or five pages before annexures.

1. Reference the application number, the examination report date, and a one-line summary of the objection.
2. Describe the applicant and the mark in two paragraphs.
3. Respond to each limb under its own heading.
4. Offer alternatives if the client can live with them: a disclaimer on a descriptive element, a limitation of the specification, or a condition of use.
5. Request a hearing in the event the objection is not waived. Always.

## Timing

The reply is due within one month of receipt of the examination report, and that period cannot be extended. If the deadline is close and the evidence is not ready, file the reply with the legal argument and state that user evidence is being compiled and will be produced at the hearing. That is a weaker position than filing complete. It is far better than an abandoned application.

> The reply is not where you prove the mark is good. It is where you make it impossible for the examiner to say no without a hearing.
